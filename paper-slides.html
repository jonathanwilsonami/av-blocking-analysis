Blocked: Autonomous-Vehicle Blocking Incidents in San Francisco

Frequency, hazard classification, and risk-matrix placement from the 2025 DEM incident log

Jonathan Wilson
Chujiang (CJ) Wu

2026-10-04

Introduction

Motivation and questions

  • Driverless robotaxis that cannot resolve a situation stop and wait. That is safe for occupants, but the stopped AV can block lanes, transit, crosswalks, and emergency vehicles.
  • San Francisco DEM logged 123 blocking incidents, February 1, 2025 – October 31, 2025.
  • Questions:
    1. How often do incidents, and each hazard type, occur?
    2. What hazards do they create, and how can narratives be classified defensibly?
    3. Where does each hazard sit on a 5×5 risk matrix, and is anything changing?

Data credit: SF DEM; original cleaning by Dr. Missy Cummings.

Data

Data at a glance

  • 123 incidents; 120 in the 6 fully covered months (184 days).
  • Feb (3 incidents, days 1–3) and Apr–May (absent) are coverage gaps, not zeros, so they are excluded from rates.
  • 67% of narratives are truncated at about 50 characters.
  • Durations: median 23 min (95% CI 17–36), 90th percentile 125 min, max 603 min.
  • Secondary sheets add ETA, neighborhood, and responder/transit flags, plus 8 extra incidents without times.

Hazard taxonomy and narrative classification

Hazard taxonomy

Code Hazard What the stopped AV obstructed / caused
H1 AV-involved collision contact with a vehicle, person, or animal
H2 Emergency-response obstruction medics, fire, police, or an active emergency scene
H3 Transit / rail obstruction Muni bus/rail, cable car, tracks
H4 Pedestrian / accessibility crosswalk, curb ramp, bike lane
H5 Occupant-related asleep/unresponsive rider, door left open, trapped occupant
H6 Multi-AV gridlock 2+ AVs stuck together (common-mode failure)
H7 Single-AV obstruction general traffic only (default)
  • Consequence, not cause. The cause (fault, dark signal, passenger, …) is coded on a separate axis.
  • Severity-first precedence (H1 > … > H7) makes the classes mutually exclusive, so frequencies add up.

Why rules instead of XGBoost or a transformer?

  • 123 short, truncated narratives in dispatcher shorthand; rare classes have 4–9 examples.
  • Learned models (cross-validated) barely beat the majority baseline. Logistic regression has F1 = 0 on emergency obstruction.
  • Zero-shot NLI: BART-MNLI 0.4, DeBERTa-v3 0.32 (not significantly different). Both call about a third of incidents “collision”.
  • Embedding similarity is the best label-free method (0.62; significantly better than BART), but over-predicts transit and pedestrian hazards.
  • Rules agree with the hand-coded audit at \(\kappa\) = 0.99. This is a consistency check: the same analyst wrote both.
  • A blind LLM second coder (Codex, given only a written guide) matches the audit at \(\kappa\) = 0.95 (macro-F1 0.95). The taxonomy can be applied consistently.
  • Selected: transparent, auditable, reproducible rules, with the LLM as a cross-check.

Impact scoring

Scoring the impact of each incident

Figure 1: Number of incidents by hazard and incident-level impact (all 123 incidents).

Impact = max(hazard consequence, duration band, cluster size). 1 Insignificant (<10 min) · 2 Minor (10–60 min, 2–4 AVs) · 3 Significant (≥60 min, transit, pedestrian path, 5+ AVs) · 4 Major (emergency obstruction, collision without injury) · 5 Severe (life-threatening emergency obstructed, injury).

Frequency estimation

How often does each hazard occur?

Figure 2: Posterior mean frequency per 30 days with 95% Jeffreys credible intervals (log scale).

Poisson rate \(\lambda\) over \(T\) = 184 days; Jeffreys posterior \(\lambda\mid n \sim \text{Gamma}(n+\tfrac12, T)\); \(P(N_{30}\ge 1) = 1-\big(\tfrac{T}{T+30}\big)^{n+1/2}\). All incidents: 19.6 per 30 days (16.2–23.4), one every 1.5 days.

Why a Bayesian probability for the matrix?

  • Answers the matrix’s question: the chance of ≥1 incident in the next 30 days, a statement about future events. Plugging in the estimate \(n/T\) treats the rate as known.
  • Carries uncertainty: the key hazards rest on 3–6 incidents, with rates uncertain by about a factor of five.
  • Sensible with few or zero events: never “probability 0” just because a hazard hasn’t been seen yet.
  • Direct statements: “41% chance of a life-threatening emergency obstruction within 30 days.”
  • Objective prior (Jeffreys) and extensible (mileage, other cities).
  • Honest caveat: the plug-in version gives the same placements here. The choice is principled, not decisive.

Is anything changing over time?

No detectable trend in rate or duration

Figure 3: Monthly incident rate per 30 days with exact 95% Poisson intervals. Gray bands mark months absent from (or only partially present in) the log.

No detectable trend. Mann–Kendall exact p = 0.86 · Poisson GLM rate ratio 0.983/month (p = 0.67) · durations: Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.23, Jonckheere–Terpstra p = 0.19. The class Mar-vs-Oct Mann–Whitney replicates: W = 377.5, p = 0.064.

The hazard mix shift is a reporting artifact

Figure 4: Share of each period’s incidents by hazard class.

The mix differs (p = 0.007), but narratives went from 98% truncated to 41% truncated at the same time. The combined H1–H5 share is unchanged (p = 0.22), and the multi-AV share (a structured field) has no trend (p = 0.17). The shift is not evidence of changed AV behavior.

Figure 5: 5×5 risk matrix of AV blocking hazards. Probability is the Jeffreys posterior-predictive probability of at least one qualifying incident in the next 30 days. Impact is the governing tier of each hazard’s risk curve.

Two kinds of red: H1 and H2 are consequence-driven (safety problems). H7 is volume-driven: its worst outcome is a traffic delay, but it happens every ~11 days (an operational problem).

Discussion and limitations

Measured vs judged

  • Measured: counts, durations, rates and intervals, trend tests, agreement scores.
  • Judged: the taxonomy, the impact rubric (60 min = Significant), the 30-day horizon, the template’s bands, and the placement rule. All were fixed before the matrix was drawn and are written in code.
  • The colors are a transparent, structured opinion built on measured rates; the sensitivity tables show how far each choice moves them.

Limitations

  • No exposure (miles/trips): rates are per calendar day. A growing fleet with a flat rate means a falling per-mile rate.
  • Under-recording: only incidents that reached DEM appear, so frequencies are lower bounds.
  • Truncated narratives push incidents into the H7 default, so H1–H5 are likely undercounted.
  • One analyst built the taxonomy, rules, and audit labels. A blind LLM second coder agreed at \(\kappa\) = 0.95; a human second coder would be stronger evidence.
  • Risk matrices are coarse (Cox 2008), so the rates, intervals, and risk curves are published alongside each cell.

Conclusions

Take-aways

  1. Blocking is routine: about 4.6 incidents per week; median 23 min; 28% last 60+ minutes.
  2. The highest risks are H1 collisions, H2 emergency obstruction, and H7 routine blockage (all Very high).
  3. No detectable trend in rate, duration, or multi-AV share. The hazard-mix shift is a recording artifact.
  4. Levers: better data (full narratives, exposure) and faster recovery, especially near emergency scenes.
